## About This Guide

- **Distillation principle**: This is not a chapter-by-chapter summary. Only methods and scripts parents can use the same day are kept; concepts are compiled from publicly known, common-knowledge methods — nothing is invented.
- **How to use**: Look up your problem in the quick-reference table below, then go straight to that book's "techniques you can use today."
- **Mindset**: No single method is the only right answer. Temperament and family context vary; try one technique, observe for a few days, then adjust — that works better than changing everything at once.
- **Boundary**: This guide does not provide medical diagnosis or treatment. For development- or health-related concerns, consult a professional.

## 《如何说孩子才会听，怎么听孩子才肯说》*How to Talk So Kids Will Listen & Listen So Kids Will Talk* (Adele Faber & Elaine Mazlish)

- **Core method**: Replace commands, lectures, and blame with "acknowledge feelings + describe instead of judge + solve problems together," turning the child from the accused into a problem-solver.
- **Age range**: All ages (most direct effect ages 2–12; the "alternatives to punishment" chapters suit ages 3+).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Name the feeling instead of denying it**: Child says "I hate my brother" → "Sometimes your brother really annoys you, huh" — not "Don't say that, he's your brother."
  2. **Describe instead of accuse**: Toys all over the floor → "The blocks are on the floor" — not "Why do you always trash the place?"
  3. **Give information instead of nagging**: "Milk spoils if it's left out" — not "How many times have I told you to put the milk back?"
  4. **Say it with one word**: At the door: "Shoes." — not "We're late again, why aren't your shoes on, I always have to push you."
  5. **Write a note**: For recurring reminders (screen time), stick a note by the TV: "One episode, then off — deal? Love, Dad."
- **One-line essence**: When a child's feelings are accepted, behavior improves; deal with the feeling first, the matter second.

## 《正面管教》*Positive Discipline* (Jane Nelsen)

- **Core method**: Kind **and** firm at once — respectful toward the child, unmoving on limits; build responsibility through logical consequences, family meetings, and curiosity questions instead of punishment or permissiveness.
- **Age range**: All ages (family meetings suit 4+; tool-style techniques work from age 2).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Kind-and-firm formula**: Child tantrums over a toy in a store → "I love you, **and** we're not buying a toy today" — calm tone, unchanged limit.
  2. **Curiosity questions instead of commands**: "What's your plan for homework? Which subject first?" — instead of "Go do your homework now."
  3. **Limited choices**: Child won't dress → "The red one or the blue one — you pick." Both options must be acceptable to you.
  4. **Family meeting**: 20 fixed minutes weekly: compliments → topic → whole-family brainstorm → pick one solution to try for a week (children 4+ can join).
  5. **Mistakes as learning**: After spilled milk → "No problem. How do you plan to clean it up?" Make repair the exercise, not the verdict.
- **One-line essence**: Children do better when they feel better; punishment makes children pay for the past, discipline teaches them to learn from mistakes.

## 《游戏力》*Playful Parenting* (Lawrence Cohen)

- **Core method**: Play is a child's first language. Use silliness, roughhousing, and role reversal to rebuild connection and dissolve tension — the "cup" metaphor: children act out when their cup of connection runs low; play refills it.
- **Age range**: Primarily 0–6 (role-reversal games suit 3–8; mind safety with roughhousing at young ages).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Role reversal**: Child fears shots → play "child is the doctor, parent is the scared patient" and ham it up ("I'm so scared!") — laughter restores control.
  2. **Let a toy speak for the child**: Won't brush teeth → pick up a puppet in a silly voice: "I'm the cavity germ! I'm terrified of toothbrushes! Whatever you do, don't brush!" Most kids will laugh and brush.
  3. **Follow their game**: 10–15 minutes of daily "playtime," timer set, child in charge; your only job is to play fully — phone away.
  4. **Counter-will games**: Morning sock standoff → "There's no way this sock will go on!" then strain and "fail" — many kids will giggle and pull it on themselves.
  5. **Roughhousing to discharge energy**: Before bed when wired → 5 minutes of "palm push" or gentle pillow bump with rules and a stop signal, then start the wind-down.
- **One-line essence**: Laughter lubricates the relationship — joining a child's world works better than dragging them into yours.

## 《全脑教养法》*The Whole-Brain Child* (Daniel Siegel & Tina Payne Bryson)

- **Core method**: Integrate left/right and up/down brain: connect on the emotional right brain first, then redirect with the logical left brain; use "name it to tame it" and storytelling to help a child integrate experience.
- **Age range**: All ages (storytelling suits 2+; the upstairs/downstairs brain idea helps parents of 2–12).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Connect right, redirect left**: During a meltdown, get down, empathize ("You're really upset"), and reason only after calm — logic mid-storm doesn't land.
  2. **Name it to tame it**: "You're frustrated and angry because the tower fell" — naming an emotion lowers its intensity.
  3. **Retell the story (narrate)**: After a scare (a fall), invite the child to tell "what happened" once calm — the storytelling itself is integrating.
  4. **Fist model of the brain**: Curl your hand into a fist: the thumb is the emotional brain, the fingers are the calm "lid." A meltdown = "flipped lid." Later you can say, "Your lid flipped — let's get it back on."
  5. **Feelings chart**: Post a faces chart on the fridge; after dinner ask the child to point at "today's face" to build emotional awareness.
- **One-line essence**: Survive first, thrive second — an integrated brain grows from integrated experience.

## 《去情绪化管教》*No-Drama Discipline* (Daniel Siegel & Tina Payne Bryson)

- **Core method**: Discipline is teaching, not punishing. Use "connect → redirect": connect emotionally to calm the child, then redirect to reflection and repair.
- **Age range**: All ages (connect-and-redirect works from ~18 months; reflective questions suit 3+).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Connect before you correct**: After hitting, first kneel, soften your voice — "I'm here, let's calm down together." A calm brain can learn; an alarmed one can't.
  2. **Less talk than you think**: Mid-storm, shrink sentences: "Stop. Breathe." Long lectures don't land in a storm.
  3. **Reflective questions** (once calm): "What happened?" "How did you feel?" "What could you do next time?" Let the answer come from the child.
  4. **Repair the harm**: Child knocked over sister's blocks → guide an action: help rebuild, or say a repair sentence. Doing beats a forced "sorry."
  5. **Parent time-out**: When you feel your own lid flipping — "I need two minutes to calm down, then we'll talk." You're modeling emotion management.
- **One-line essence**: Before disciplining ask: Why did my child act this way? What do I want to teach in this moment? What's the best way to teach it?

## 《Good Inside》*Good Inside* (Becky Kennedy)

- **Core method**: Behavior problems are signals of unmet needs; "the most defiant child is the child who most needs to be seen." Set limits while staying connected — firm and kind at once.
- **Age range**: All ages (meltdown tools suit 2–8; the repair chapters are for parents themselves).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Behavior is a signal, not a verdict**: Child yells "I hate you!" → internally translate: "My feelings are too big." Respond: "You're really angry. I'm right here."
  2. **Two-part limit**: "I won't let you hit your sister (limit). You can be angry — I'll stay with you while the anger passes (connection)."
  3. **Script hard moments in advance**: Before the store: "If you see candy you really want, we can take a photo for the wish list; we're not buying today. Tell me the plan back."
  4. **Parent repair**: "I yelled this morning. That wasn't your fault — I didn't manage my own feelings. I'd like a hug and a restart." It's never too late.
  5. **Wish list**: Child insists on a toy → "Let's take a picture and put it on your birthday wish list." Recorded wants lose urgency.
- **One-line essence**: A child's most unlovable moment is when they most need love — you can hold the limit while the child stays sure they're a good kid.

## 《自驱型成长》*The Self-Driven Child* (William Stixrud & Ned Johnson)

- **Core method**: Be a consultant, not a boss. A sense of control is the antidote to stress and the fuel of motivation — hand decisions back to the child step by step.
- **Age range**: 6–12 focus (homework, activities, screen chapters); the consultant stance also helps with preschoolers.
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Consultant script**: "Homework is your job. What do you need from me — help planning, or a quiet room?" — instead of "Do your homework or I'm taking the tablet."
  2. **"You decide" ladder**: Start small (tomorrow's outfit, practice before or after play), then widen: "You set tonight's study plan; I'm just an advisor."
  3. **Stress conversation opener**: "What worries you most about the test?" Reflect the worry back, then problem-solve — not "There's nothing to be scared of."
  4. **Homework ownership transfer**: List together what's fully the child's, what needs you; post it by the desk.
  5. **Calm home first**: A family-wide screen-free window (e.g., 30 minutes after dinner) that parents also follow — modeling self-control beats lecturing.
- **One-line essence**: Kids don't do well because they'll feel good later; they do well when they feel good now. Take your hand off the wheel a little so the child can learn to steer.

## 《P.E.T.父母效能训练》*P.E.T. Parent Effectiveness Training* (Thomas Gordon)

- **Core method**: Determine problem ownership — whose need is unmet? If it's the child's, listen receptively; if it's yours, send an I-message; if both, use the no-lose Method III negotiation.
- **Age range**: All ages (listening and I-messages from age 2; Method III negotiation suits 4+).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Check ownership first**: Child is frustrated that blocks keep falling — that's the child's problem. Resist giving advice; listen first.
  2. **Door openers**: "I don't want to go to school" → "Oh? Tell me more." No "You have to go" verdict.
  3. **Active listening**: "Jake won't play with me" → "You're sad — and kind of mad too, huh?" Understanding precedes teaching.
  4. **Three-part I-message**: Child bouncing a ball over the neighbor's nap — "Aunt Li downstairs is napping (behavior), the thumping goes straight down (effect), and I'm worried about complaints (feeling)" — no "You're so noisy."
  5. **Method III, six steps**: Define the problem → both list ideas (wild ones welcome) → evaluate together → choose one → try it → review after a few days. Works for the "screen time" war.
- **One-line essence**: Parents don't have to win and children don't have to lose; when both needs are on the table, a third answer usually appears.

## 《非暴力沟通》*Nonviolent Communication* (Marshall Rosenberg) — parenting lens

- **Core method**: Observation → feeling → need → request. Describe behavior without judgment, express your own feelings and needs honestly, and make a concrete, doable request; listen for the need behind the child's behavior.
- **Age range**: All ages (parent self-expression from age 2; guiding children to express needs suits 3+).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Observation, not judgment**: "Toys were left out three days this week" — not "You never clean up."
  2. **Feeling + need sentence**: Child interrupts your call → "When my call gets interrupted I get anxious, because I need to finish this" — not "How rude."
  3. **Make requests doable**: Instead of "Be good" — "While I'm on the phone, you can draw; come find me when I'm done. Deal?"
  4. **Translate attacks**: "You're a bad mommy!" — translate internally as "I have an unseen need": "You're really angry. Do you need me to play with you?"
  5. **Listen for the need**: Won't wear the coat → "Is it because you're hot, or because this one feels scratchy?" Once the need is found, the fix is often easy.
- **One-line essence**: Criticism earns defense; observation, feeling, need, and request create the chance for connection.

## 《捕捉儿童敏感期》*Capturing Children's Sensitive Periods* (Sun Ruixue, Montessori lens)

- **Core method**: Children pass through intense "sensitive periods" (mouth, hands, order, language, small objects); supporting the period works better than correcting the "problem behavior."
- **Age range**: 0–6 (mouth/hands 0–2; order 1–3; small objects 1–4; language 0–6).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Don't forcibly stop hand-mouthing (0–1)**: Keep hands clean and offer teethers instead of repeatedly interrupting oral exploration.
  2. **Honor "do-overs" in the order-sensitive period**: If the child insists "Mommy must open the door!" or "I press the elevator button!" — redo it when you can. Order is an expression of security, not defiance.
  3. **Make room for small-object watching (1–4)**: Pebbles, ants, a hair on the floor — leave time on walks to wait and watch; don't rush.
  4. **Soothe after broken order**: Meltdown over the wrong cup or a changed route → "It's different from what you expected, and that's hard." Restore what you can (the original cup); accompany what you can't.
  5. **Real tools in the movement-sensitive period (2+)**: When children want to sweep, cut fruit, pour water — give a small broom, a safe knife, a little pitcher, and let them actually do it. "Help me do it" is a request from development.
- **One-line essence**: A sensitive period is a window development opens by itself; see it and support it, and many "problem behaviors" turn out to be development itself.

## 《1-2-3 Magic》*1-2-3 Magic* (Thomas Phelan)

- **Core method**: Counting manages "stop-behaviors" (whining, teasing, tantrums): no talking, no emotion — count 1, 2, 3 and follow through with a pre-agreed rest. "Start-behaviors" (homework, mornings) get positive tactics: praise and timers.
- **Age range**: 2–12 (counting from age 2; start-behavior tactics are most used with school-age kids).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Standard count**: Tantrum-demand → calmly raise fingers: "That's 1." Pause. "That's 2." "That's 3." No words, no lecture, no anger in between.
  2. **Pre-agreed consequence at 3**: When calm (not mid-storm), agree on what "3" means (5 minutes of rest, no show today) — enforce calmly, and the whole household stays consistent.
  3. **No-explanation rule**: If the child protests "That's not fair!" mid-count — don't debate, keep counting. Explanation happens later, when calm.
  4. **Outsource nagging to a timer**: "We'll set the kitchen timer for 10 minutes — dressed before the bell." The timer, not the parent, plays the nag.
  5. **Positive start-behavior trio**: Small task + timer + specific praise on completion — instead of repeat nagging.
- **One-line essence**: Count like a robot — the less you say and the calmer you are, the clearer the limit.

## 《忍住！别插手》*Hold Back! Don't Step In* (Takatori Shizuka & JAM Japan)

- **Core method**: Self-care grows from non-interference — step back, break tasks (tidying, time management, chores) into steps, tolerate slow and messy, and let checklists and charts replace verbal nagging.
- **Age range**: 3–12 (tidying from 3; time-management training from 5 as the focus).
- **Techniques you can use today**:
  1. **Three-step tidying**: Tidy = decide where things live → put back after use → check weekly. Give every category of toy a "home," labeled with pictures (works before reading).
  2. **Morning routine chart, not nagging**: Draw the morning as a 5-box picture list (wake, dress, brush, eat, shoes). Ask only: "Which boxes are left?"
  3. **Count 10 before helping**: When shoelaces are slow, count to 10 silently before speaking; ask "Want help?" before your hands move.
  4. **Make time visible**: Before clock-reading, use an hourglass or kitchen timer so "ten more minutes" is something you can watch — the tool reminds, not the parent's voice.
  5. **Visible chore chart**: Post the family chore chart on the fridge; everyone claims slots, and the child adds a magnet to their own box. Let the chart talk.
- **One-line essence**: A child's abilities grow where a parent's hands step back — "don't step in" isn't neglect; it's handing over the task, broken into steps.
