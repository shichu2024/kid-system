# Common Parenting Problem Navigation (problem-navigation)

> Purpose: a "triage desk + first-aid manual" for high-frequency parenting problems. Each problem follows the structure **What you see → Layered response (L1/L2) → Deep resources → Red flags**.
> This file offers caregiving guidance and seek-care prompts only — **it is not a medical diagnosis**. A red flag means "an evaluation by a pediatrician or child-development professional is recommended", never a diagnostic conclusion.
> Resource references use relative paths within this repository: knowledge/ files are referenced directly (zh files are the primary source, e.g. `../books-guide.md`); age-based data lives in `../../references/en/`.

---

## Triage Principles (before any strategy)

Before addressing any problem, pass it through three screens in order:

1. **Rule out physiological causes first**: Is the child hungry / tired / too hot or cold / unwell (teething, ear pain, constipation, itchy allergy)? A large share of "behavior problems" are really physical discomfort. Check wake windows in `../../references/en/sleep-guide.json` and feeding frequency in `../../references/en/feeding-stages.json`; fix sleep and food before addressing behavior.
2. **Check developmental appropriateness**: Is this behavior typical for the age? Compare against the four domains in `../../references/en/milestones.json`. For example, grabbing toys and refusing to share at age 2 is age-appropriate; most "lying" before 3 is imagination blending with reality. Age-appropriate behavior does not need "correcting" — it needs guidance and time.
3. **Only then apply strategies**: Once you have confirmed the cause is neither physical nor far ahead of / behind milestones, use the L1/L2 responses below. L1 = in-the-moment actions while it is happening (usable today); L2 = habit-level strategies over days to weeks.

If any **red flag** appears at any point, act on the red flag first; strategies can wait.

## 1. Emotions & Behavior

### 1.1 Tantrums

- **What you see**: Peaks at ages 1-4. Screaming, crying, dropping to the floor, kicking — often triggered by trivial things (a broken cracker, the wrong cup color). Reasoning goes nowhere.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Silently run the triage screen (hungry? tired? unwell?) — tantrums cluster around fatigue and hunger windows;
  - Get down to eye level and empathize in few words: "You're angry because the tower fell";
  - Keep the child safe (move sharp objects), stay present, no isolating, no yelling, no lecturing mid-meltdown;
  - Debrief after calm, using role-play or puppets to reenact.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Log timing and triggers (use the journal skill; see `../../references/knowledge-index.json` for the data index) and intervene ahead of the pattern (snack before outings, avoid the tired window);
  - Teach emotion vocabulary: emotion-naming games with picture books and feeling cards;
  - Offer a safe outlet for anger: punching a cushion, tearing scrap paper, stomping while counting to 10.
- **Deep resources**: `../books-guide.md`, The Whole-Brain Child chapter (connect before redirect; name it to tame it); Playful Parenting chapter (turning standoffs into games); `../communication-playbook.md`, reflective listening section.
- **Red flags**: Extremely frequent tantrums (many per day for weeks) intense enough to injure self or others; tantrums still the main mode of expression after age 5-6; accompanied by marked language or social delays versus milestones — an evaluation by a pediatrician (child health clinic) is recommended.

### 1.2 Hitting / Biting

- **What you see**: Common at ages 1-3. Strikes or bites when excited, frustrated, or trying to socialize; the child may not grasp that they caused pain.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Stop it immediately, calmly and firmly: "No hitting. Hitting hurts" — serious tone, no yelling;
  - Attend to the hurt child first (model caring for the victim), then respond briefly to the aggressor; don't amplify with an audience or a lecture;
  - Name the positive intent: "You wanted to play with him, right? We can wave or offer a toy."
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Teach substitute actions: express wants with words / offering an object / a gentle pat; rehearse "gentle touch" at home;
  - Observe triggers (contested toys, language lagging behind emotion, over-excitement) and reduce them;
  - Ensure enough gross-motor activity daily via outdoor games in `../../references/en/games-library.json`.
- **Deep resources**: `../books-guide.md`, Positive Discipline chapter (kind and firm; mistakes as learning); `../../references/en/milestones.json` domains.social_cognitive (emotion and behavior guidance).
- **Red flags**: Frequent unprovoked attacks persisting 2-3 months without improvement; aggression with self-injury; near-absent eye contact and affection — an evaluation by a pediatrician is recommended.

### 1.3 Grabbing Toys / Refusing to Share

- **What you see**: Peaks at ages 2-3. Clutching toys, pushing, shouting "Mine!" Parents often feel pressured to "teach sharing".
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Referee, don't judge: describe the situation rather than ruling who is right — "You both want the car";
  - Offer a turn-taking plan with a timer (hourglass or counting to 20); "taking turns" is far more graspable for toddlers than "sharing";
  - Public toys: first-come plus waiting in line; for their own toys, allow the child to say "not lending it".
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Ownership language: "This is yours — you decide" + "That's his; let's ask him";
  - Model borrowing at home: "May I borrow this? I'll give it right back" — and actually return it;
  - Before guests arrive, sort toys together into "happy to share" and "special toys that go away".
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/milestones.json` domains.social_cognitive (development of ownership and turn-taking); `../books-guide.md`, Playful Parenting chapter.
- **Red flags**: Only when accompanied by persistent social withdrawal or near-total ignoring of peers (clear social milestone delay) is an evaluation recommended; "not sharing" alone is not a problem.

### 1.4 Lying

- **What you see**: Peaks at ages 3-5. "It wasn't me" after spilling milk; inventing "there was a dinosaur at preschool today". Most young-child "lies" are wishful thinking, imagination mixed with reality, or fear of punishment — not a moral failure.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - No trap questions (asking "Did you do it?" when you know forces a second lie);
  - State facts and offer a path: "The milk spilled. Let's get a cloth";
  - For fiction, neither expose nor celebrate: "That's a story you made up — tell me more!"
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Play "real or made up": take turns saying sentences for the other to guess;
  - Lower the cost of honesty: keep repeating "Telling the truth won't get you punished; hiding it is the trouble";
  - Model honesty yourself; avoid demonstrative white lies ("The police will come if you lie again").
- **Deep resources**: `../books-guide.md`, The Whole-Brain Child chapter (development of imagination vs. reality); `../communication-playbook.md`, questioning and dialogue section.
- **Red flags**: After age 6, frequent lying combined with stealing or hurting behavior, or persistently flat, cold affect — a child-psychology professional evaluation is recommended. Occasional preschool lying is developmentally normal.

### 1.5 Dawdling

- **What you see**: Ten minutes to put on shoes, food held in the mouth without chewing, endless nagging around tooth-brushing. The more you rush, the slower they get; it ends in shouting.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Swap "hurry up" for a concrete first step: "Sit on the shoe bench" beats "Put your shoes on NOW";
  - Use a visible timer (hourglass or phone countdown): "When the sand runs out, we leave";
  - Gamify: races and role-play ("The little train is leaving — all passengers aboard!").
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Break routines into a visual chart: photo-based "Four Steps Out the Door" by the entryway;
  - Build a "dawdling budget": shift all schedules 15 minutes earlier to defuse parental anxiety;
  - Let age-appropriate natural consequences speak: dawdling means one fewer bedtime story — not added punishment.
- **Deep resources**: `../books-guide.md`, Playful Parenting chapter (turning tasks into games); `../quick-rescue-games.md`, out-the-door and transition games.
- **Red flags**: Dawdling with pervasive difficulty following instructions, little eye contact, and delayed language comprehension — compare against `../../references/en/milestones.json` domains.language; if clearly behind, an evaluation by a pediatrician is recommended.

### 1.6 Separation Anxiety

- **What you see**: Intensifies from 8-18 months. Meltdowns the moment a parent leaves sight, clinging to legs, needing one specific person at bedtime. A normal feature of attachment development.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Always say goodbye — never sneak away (it breaks trust and worsens anxiety);
  - A short, fixed goodbye ritual: one hug + one sentence + one wave, then leave decisively;
  - Give a return time the child can grasp: "After your afternoon snack, Mommy will be back."
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Practice trust with graded separations: 1 min → 5 min → 30 min, always returning on time;
  - A transition object: a scarf with a parent's scent or a fixed comfort toy;
  - Preview separation-and-reunion with picture books (see the separation-anxiety list in `../picture-books.md`).
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/milestones.json` domains.social_cognitive (attachment and separation); `../picture-books.md`, themed booklists.
- **Red flags**: Extreme distress at separation persisting beyond 1 month past age 3 without easing, with food refusal or severe sleep disruption, or total inability to be with anyone outside the family — an evaluation by a pediatrician or child-psychology professional is recommended.

### 1.7 Night Terrors & Nightmares

- **What you see**: **Night terrors** mostly occur 1-3 hours after falling asleep: the child suddenly sits up screaming, eyes open but blank, doesn't recognize people, hard to console, no memory the next morning. **Nightmares** occur later at night: the child wakes crying, can describe the scary content, and seeks comfort.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Night terrors: do not force waking (it adds confusion); keep safe, dim the light, stay quietly nearby until it passes (usually 10-20 min);
  - Nightmares: hold and comfort, validate the fear ("that dream was really scary"), don't dismiss or mock; small night-light, stay until back to sleep;
  - Note the clock time — terrors at a fixed hour can be disrupted with a brief gentle wake 15 minutes earlier (see L2).
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Terrors correlate with sleep debt and chaotic schedules: check age-based totals in `../../references/en/sleep-guide.json` and restore sleep first;
  - Remove scary stimulation in the last hour before bed (scary stories, rough play, startling videos);
  - For frequent nightmares, play "rewrite the ending" in the daytime: draw the dream and add a "super helper" to the ending.
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/sleep-guide.json` bands[] and principles; `../quick-rescue-games.md`, bedtime calming games.
- **Red flags**: Night terrors nearly every night with daytime sleepiness, heavy snoring, or breathing-pause-like episodes; or nightmare content involving real traumatic events — for the former, a pediatric/sleep-specialist evaluation is recommended; for the latter, a child-psychology professional evaluation is recommended.

### 1.8 Screen-Time Battles

- **What you see**: Once on a phone or tablet, it can't be turned off; switching it off triggers a tantrum; needs videos to eat, needs a phone outside.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Preview + countdown: "When this episode ends, we turn it off" causes far less conflict than a sudden shutdown;
  - The moment the screen goes off, hand over a substitute: a specific game or small task as a transition hook;
  - If emotions flare, apply the tantrum L1 — and don't restore the screen to stop crying (that reinforces crying).
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Family rules for everyone (parents included): fixed windows and duration; screen-free at meals and in the hour before bed;
  - Stock substitutes: screen-free games by scene (cooking, car rides, queues) from `../quick-rescue-games.md` and `../../references/en/games-library.json`;
  - When screens are used, watch together and talk about it — turning "viewing" into interaction; never the default soother.
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/games-library.json` (screen-substitute game index); `../books-guide.md`, Playful Parenting chapter.
- **Red flags**: Screens become the only interest; weeks of dysregulation after removal; language/social development lagging milestones — an evaluation by a pediatrician is recommended.

## 2. Feeding & Nutrition

### 2.1 Picky Eating / Food Refusal

- **What you see**: Refuses whole categories (vegetables/meat), eats only white rice or noodles; turns away from new foods, clenches the mouth, even gags.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - No forcing, no bribing ("dessert if you eat the broccoli" elevates dessert and devalues broccoli);
  - One tiny portion of the new food at the edge of the plate; whether to eat it is the child's call;
  - End the meal calmly after 20-30 minutes; no chasing with a spoon.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - The "15-exposure rule": keep presenting the same food in different forms (steamed/roasted/cut into shapes) — familiarity first, swallowing later;
  - Swap within the category to protect nutrition: use the substitution logic in the food-category table in `../../references/en/nutrition-standards.md` (no broccoli → cauliflower or bok choy; no pork loin → chicken thigh or tofu);
  - Involve the child in shopping, washing, and plating — "I made it, I want to taste it".
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/nutrition-standards.md` (same-category swaps and variety); `../../references/en/feeding-stages.json` stages[] (age-based portion baselines — to tell true undereating from high parental expectations).
- **Red flags**: Weight/height growth curve falling across two consecutive check-ups; allergy-like avoidance of whole food groups (hives, swollen lips); chronic post-meal vomiting or choking on swallows — a pediatric/child-health evaluation is recommended.

### 2.2 Playing During Meals

- **What you see**: Leaves the table eight times per meal; fed while chasing around; eats only with a video on; a meal takes an hour.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Fixed chair and seat: leaving the table means the meal ends ("You got down — are you done?" confirm, then clear the food);
  - Remove competitors: screens off, toys off the table;
  - Parents stop chase-feeding — chasing trains "play now, get fed later".
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Set the family rhythm: three meals and two snacks at fixed times; only water between (see 2.4);
  - Nothing but water in the 1.5 hours before meals — let hunger join the table;
  - Meal rituals: set the table together, a starting song, the whole family seated for 20 minutes.
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/feeding-stages.json` stages[].frequency (age-based meal structure); `../books-guide.md`, Positive Discipline chapter (kind-and-firm limit enforcement).
- **Red flags**: By itself a habit issue; if growth curves decline or oral-motor signs appear (weak chewing, frequent choking), an evaluation by a child-health clinic is recommended.

### 2.3 Appetite Fluctuations

- **What you see**: Two bowls yesterday, half a bowl today; ravenous for a stretch, then no appetite. Parents worry about "food accumulation" or illness.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Triage first (listless? fever? mouth ulcers? teething?); if all clear, calmly accept "a smaller eating day";
  - Don't backfill with snacks or sweetened milk "to make up nutrition" — the single biggest killer of healthy fluctuation.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Judge intake by the week, not the meal: keep a week of records (via the journal skill); most fluctuations balance out;
  - Compare against the age-based baselines in `../../references/en/feeding-stages.json` stages[].amount to check whether "eating little" is only relative to parental expectation;
  - The growth curve is the gold standard: tracking along the child's own curve = intake is sufficient.
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/feeding-stages.json` (age-based amounts and frequency); `../../references/en/nutrition-standards.md` (age-based energy and nutrients).
- **Red flags**: Poor appetite over 2 weeks with low energy, weight plateau or loss, fever, or vomiting — see a pediatrician.

### 2.4 Snack Demands

- **What you see**: Constant requests for candy/cookies/chips; crying as leverage; no appetite at meals but "starving" between them.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - No bargaining mid-crying: empathize and restate the rule ("You really want it. Snack time comes with the afternoon snack");
  - Offer bounded choice: "Right now it's apple or yogurt — you pick."
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - A fixed snack schedule: one mid-morning and one mid-afternoon slot, small portions, ≥1.5 hours from meals;
  - Visibility management at home: keep sugary/salty snacks out of the house or in opaque cupboards; fruit and yogurt within child reach;
  - Family-wide consistency, including grandparents "secretly giving in" (record family-rule conflicts in the rules library to align).
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/nutrition-standards.md` (role of snacks in the age-based dietary pattern and recommended options).
- **Red flags**: Compulsive craving for specific foods plus repeated hives/vomiting/diarrhea after eating warrants an allergy-focused pediatric evaluation; simply liking snacks is not a red flag.

### 2.5 Self-Feeding Regression

- **What you see**: Used to spoon-feed themselves or eat by hand, now demands feeding, throws the spoon, or accepts only purees and refuses textures.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Triage first: teething pain? Mouth ulcer? Recent illness? New sibling or new environment? Regression is often a bid for comfort or attention;
  - Skip "You used to be able to do this"; offer brief gentle help (scoop half), while keeping some part they do themselves.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Lower the difficulty ladder: an easier-grip spoon, thicker scoopable food (thick congee, steamed chunks) to rebuild success;
  - Raise the share of finger foods, following the finger-food approach in `../../references/en/feeding-stages.json` stages[9-11m];
  - During regression: more connection outside meals, fewer corrections inside them.
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/feeding-stages.json` stages[] (skills and food textures by month age); `../books-guide.md`, The Whole-Brain Child chapter (regression as a distress signal).
- **Red flags**: Regression lasting over 1 month plus simultaneous regression in other domains (fewer words, more falls) — any multi-domain regression warrants a prompt child-health evaluation.

## 3. Sleep & Rhythm

### 3.1 Frequent Night Waking

- **What you see**: Waking 3+ times a night, or every 2 hours past 6 months; multiple night feeds; crying unless fed.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Wait 1-2 minutes before going in — see whether the child re-settles; rushing in teaches "wake = someone comes";
  - Escalate soothing gently: voice → light pat → picking up; put down once asleep;
  - Make night interactions boring: no playing, no talking, no bright lights.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Check the age-based night-sleep totals and night-feed guidance in `../../references/en/sleep-guide.json` bands[] — most "frequent waking" traces to mismatched wake windows (over- or under-tired);
  - A fixed bedtime routine (bath – massage – book – lights out, 20-30 min), same order, same time;
  - Wean night feeds gradually: shorter feeds / diluted / slower response, stepped down.
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/sleep-guide.json` bands[] + methods (settling approaches); `../quick-rescue-games.md`, bedtime calming games.
- **Red flags**: Waking with snoring, mouth breathing, or breathing-pause-like episodes, or with heavy sweating and slow growth — a pediatric/ENT evaluation is recommended; sleep disruption with developmental regression warrants a child-health evaluation.

### 3.2 Sleep Regressions / Nap Refusal

- **What you see**: A formerly good sleeper suddenly wakes constantly (peaks: 4 months, new-motor-skill periods, nap transitions); or flatly refuses the daytime nap, getting more wired as the day goes on.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Don't force the nap: switch to "quiet time" (curtains drawn, story audio, 20 minutes lying down) — protect the floor, not the nap;
  - For regression night-waking, apply 3.1's L1 consistently; don't add new sleep crutches (they outlast the regression).
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Recognize nap transitions: compare `../../references/en/sleep-guide.json` bands[].day_naps (e.g., 12-18m two-naps-to-one); forcing two naps past the transition only prolongs the battle;
  - Adjust the schedule by wake windows during regressions; give plenty of daytime practice of new skills (more crawling and cruising by day → less nighttime practicing);
  - During transitions, push the nap 30-60 minutes later and move bedtime earlier.
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/sleep-guide.json` bands[].day_naps and wake_windows.
- **Red flags**: None. Sleep regressions are developmental; if disruption lasts beyond 6 weeks with total sleep persistently below the age-based floor, consult a child-health clinic to rule out discomfort.

### 3.3 Bedtime Stalling / Early Waking

- **What you see**: "One more book / some water / need to pee" on endless loop, dragging bedtime an hour; awake at 5 a.m. before dawn, then tired all day.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Pre-pack the stall needs: build water + toilet + hug into the routine itself; afterwards, gently hold the "bedtime agreement" for new requests;
  - Give bounded ritual control: "Two books tonight — which two do you pick?" Choice reduces bargaining;
  - For early waking: pretend-sleep and low interaction at first — no lights, no play; blackout curtains; make dawn boring.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Stalling often means "bedtime = separation": add dedicated one-on-one time by day and make reunion concrete at night ("tomorrow when you wake up, we'll…");
  - Audit light and sound (blackout curtains, white noise), overly late bedtimes, and too-late naps;
  - Align the schedule with age-based totals rather than fighting the child.
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/sleep-guide.json` bands[] (age-based night/day totals); `../picture-books.md`, bedtime-themed booklist.
- **Red flags**: Chronic extreme difficulty falling asleep (over an hour lying awake) with hyperactive daytime presentation, or early waking with persistently low mood — an evaluation by a child-health clinic is recommended.

## 4. Social Skills & Habits

### 4.1 Potty Training

- **What you see**: Ages 2-4 topic. Resists the potty, withholds stool, dry by day but wet at night, or regresses to frequent accidents.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - No shaming, no drama after accidents: "Wet pants feel icky — let's change. Next time you can tell Mommy earlier";
  - If resisting the potty, don't force sitting (forcing drives stool withholding); use doll demonstrations or picture books instead;
  - Prompt by timing cues, not an alarm barrage: a calm offer 20-30 min after meals, before bed, before leaving.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Confirm readiness: walks and sits steadily, diaper dry for 2 hours, can signal the need, shows interest (see self-care milestones in `../../references/en/milestones.json` domains.social_cognitive);
  - Visualize the fixed sequence (undress – sit – flush – dress – wash) and praise specifically and immediately ("You sat on the potty yourself");
  - For regressions (new sibling, preschool start, illness), drop expectations back to diapers for a few days, then resume.
- **Deep resources**: `../../references/en/milestones.json` domains.social_cognitive (self-care and habits); `../picture-books.md`, potty-themed booklist.
- **Red flags**: Frequent daytime accidents after age 5; long-term painful, resistant stooling with hard stools (the constipation cycle); or regression persisting 2-3 months after established continence — a child-health/pediatric evaluation is recommended.

### 4.2 Sibling Rivalry

- **What you see**: The older child hits/pinches/grabs from the baby; or regresses (wants a bottle, wetting the bed, says "send the baby back"); or becomes overly "good" to win love.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Stop harmful behavior immediately and protect the baby — but don't give the older child a long scolding stage (negative attention is still attention);
  - Name the older child's feelings instead of labeling: "There's a new baby in the house. You feel sad and angry sometimes. That's okay";
  - Daily 10-15 minutes of "big-kid-only time": just them, no mention of the baby, their choice of play.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Offset "baby perks" with "big-kid privileges": 10 minutes later bedtime, the big-kid cup;
  - Assign feasible helper roles (fetching a diaper, choosing an outfit); praise the effort, never the sacrifice;
  - No comparisons and no "give in to your little brother/sister" during conflicts; accept regressions without judgment — they usually fade within weeks.
- **Deep resources**: `../books-guide.md`, How to Talk So Kids Will Listen chapter (sibling conflict mediation); `../communication-playbook.md`, sibling dialogue scripts.
- **Red flags**: Escalating harm to the baby despite consistent limits, with persistent low mood or self-injury — a child-psychology professional evaluation is recommended.

### 4.3 Preschool / Kindergarten Adjustment

- **What you see**: In the first 1-4 weeks (even 2-3 months): morning crying, clinging to legs, more night waking, accident regression, refusing to talk about school on weekends.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Short, firm goodbyes (reuse separation-anxiety L1): ritual + a pickup promise you keep — "After your afternoon snack, Mommy will be at the gate";
  - Full-attention pickup: hug first, questions later; never interrogate "Did you cry today?";
  - Allow a comfort object (small toy or family photo) at school.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Preview 1-2 months ahead: visit the school, read starting-school books, practice short separations;
  - Manage your own emotions: no teary eyes or lingering at the gate — children read parents' faces;
  - Lower expectations early on: earlier weekend bedtimes, added naps, simple food, no tutoring-style "make-up" time.
- **Deep resources**: `../picture-books.md`, starting-school booklist; `../../references/en/milestones.json` domains.social_cognitive (attachment and separation).
- **Red flags**: Intense daily crying beyond 2-3 months with persistent food refusal, night terrors, or major behavioral regression; or completely mute at school while normal at home, persisting — discuss with the school and seek a child-psychology professional evaluation.

### 4.4 Fears (Dark / Strangers / White Coats)

- **What you see**: Peaks at ages 2-6. Refuses lights-off, fears "something under the bed"; hides behind legs with strangers; melts down at the clinic.
- **L1 in the moment**:
  - Acknowledge the fear as real ("It's dark and hard to see — you're a bit scared. I'm right here"); never dismiss ("There's nothing to be scared of") or mock;
  - Never leave the child alone inside the fear "to toughen up"; only graded exposure with company works;
  - Doctor fear: bring a comfort object, play "doctor and patient" in advance, let the child hold the stethoscope and be the doctor.
- **L2 habit strategies**:
  - Darkness: night-light + a "patrol ritual" (check under the bed and in the closet together before sleep, turning fear into a game); draw the feared thing and re-draw it silly;
  - Strangers: don't force greetings or hugs; offer stepping stones (wave, blow a kiss) and model friendly small talk yourself;
  - Medical fear: normalize doll-hospital play; tell the truth before visits ("the shot hurts a tiny bit") — one lie costs all your credit.
- **Deep resources**: `../books-guide.md`, Playful Parenting chapter (role-reversal games to master fear); `../picture-books.md`, emotions-and-fears booklist.
- **Red flags**: Fear intensity and duration clearly impairing life (weeks of no sleep over darkness; freezing/meltdowns at nearly all unfamiliar settings with no easing trend) — a child-psychology professional evaluation is recommended. Stage-bound, specific fears are developmentally normal.
